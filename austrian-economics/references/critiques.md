## Critique of Keynesianism {#keynesianism}

### "Aggregate Demand" Is Not a Cause

Keynesians treat insufficient aggregate demand as the cause of recessions.
This puts the cart before the horse. Aggregate demand falls *because* the
structure of production was distorted during the boom. Malinvestments must
be liquidated and resources reallocated. Artificially boosting aggregate
demand through government spending or monetary expansion doesn't fix the
structural problem — it prevents the necessary correction.

Analogy: telling a hungover person to keep drinking. The symptoms are
temporarily suppressed, but the underlying problem worsens.

### The Paradox of Thrift Is Wrong

Keynes argued increased saving during recession worsens the downturn by
reducing spending. The Austrian response:

- Saving is what funds investment. Increased genuine saving lowers interest
  rates naturally, making previously unprofitable but *actually sustainable*
  investments viable.
- The problem during a bust isn't too much saving — it's that previous
  malinvestments must be corrected.
- If everyone saved more, the result would be a restructured, more
  capital-intensive economy that produces more in the future. This is
  what economic growth actually is.
- Keynes confused *hoarding* (increasing cash balances, which affects
  money's purchasing power) with *saving* (deferring consumption to fund
  investment, which affects the interest rate).

### The Multiplier Is Illusory

The Keynesian multiplier assumes government spending creates additional
income creating additional spending in a chain reaction. This ignores
where the government gets the money:

- **Taxation** reduces private spending dollar-for-dollar. The "multiplier"
  applies equally to the private spending that was destroyed.
- **Borrowing** crowds out private investment. Resources used by government
  are resources not available to the private sector.
- **Money creation** causes inflation and distorts relative prices. The
  additional nominal spending reflects redistributed purchasing power,
  not new wealth.

There is no net creation of wealth. The multiplier counts the government's
spending as a gain while ignoring the private sector's loss.

Bastiat's broken window fallacy applies directly: yes, the glazier gets
business from the broken window. But the money spent on glass would have
been spent on something the shop owner actually wanted.

### Liquidity Preference Confuses Money and Time

Keynes argued interest rates are determined by supply and demand for money
(liquidity preference), not time preference. The Austrian response:

- Demand for cash balances affects money's purchasing power, not the
  interest rate
- Interest is the price of time — the discount of future goods relative
  to present goods
- Conflating money demand with the interest rate leads to the error that
  increasing money supply can lower interest rates without distorting the
  production structure
- In reality, monetary expansion that pushes rates below the natural rate
  triggers the boom-bust cycle

### "Animal Spirits" Explains Nothing

Attributing business fluctuations to shifts in business confidence is
not an explanation — it's a label for the thing that needs explaining.
*Why* does confidence shift?

ABCT provides a causal mechanism: credit expansion → distorted interest
rates → malinvestment → inevitable correction. Keynesianism describes
symptoms (falling investment, rising unemployment) without explaining
their cause.

### The Keynesian Policy Ratchet

In practice, Keynesian policy is asymmetric:
- During downturns: "Stimulate! Spend! Run deficits!"
- During booms: The recommended surpluses and spending cuts never
  materialize because they're politically painful.
- Result: permanent deficit spending, growing government share of the
  economy, accumulating debt, and chronic inflationary pressure.

This isn't a failure of implementation. It's the predictable political
consequence of a theory that provides intellectual cover for spending.

## Critique of Monetarism {#monetarism}

### Money Is Not Neutral

Friedman's quantity theory (MV = PQ) treats money as affecting "the price
level" proportionally in the long run. The Austrian response:

- Money enters the economy at specific points (bank lending, government
  spending) and affects different prices at different times and magnitudes
  (Cantillon effect).
- Those who receive new money first benefit at the expense of those who
  receive it last.
- This redistributive effect doesn't wash out. It permanently changes
  the structure of ownership and production.
- The equation MV = PQ is a tautology (velocity is defined to make it
  true) dressed up as a causal theory.

### The Stable Growth Rule Is Naive

Friedman proposed the central bank increase money supply at a constant rate
(e.g., 3% per year). The Austrian critique:

- There is no "correct" rate. Any rate of money creation beyond what
  the market would produce (under commodity money) distorts relative prices.
- Even a "stable" rate creates boom-bust cycles if it exceeds real output
  growth, because it artificially depresses interest rates.
- The framework of central banking itself is the problem, not the
  particular rate chosen. Giving fallible humans control over the money
  supply is institutionalizing the source of the problem.

### Monetarism Shares the Aggregate Fixation

Both Keynesians and Monetarists think in terms of aggregate demand, the
price level, and total output. Both miss the microeconomic structure:
the pattern of relative prices, the temporal structure of production,
the specific allocation of resources across industries.

Friedman's analysis of the Great Depression — that the Fed allowed the
money supply to contract too sharply — is half right. The contraction
worsened the depression. But the depression's *cause* was the preceding
credit boom of the 1920s, which created the malinvestments that needed
correcting. Monetarism treats the bust as the problem; Austrians trace
the causation back to the boom.

## Critique of Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) {#mmt}

### Sovereign Currency Issuance Is Not a Free Lunch

MMT's claim that a sovereign currency issuer "cannot go bankrupt" is
trivially true but economically meaningless:

- Yes, a government can always print more of its own currency.
- Printing money doesn't create real resources. It redistributes existing
  resources from money-holders (via inflation) to money-printers.
- The constraint on government spending is not "running out of money" but
  real scarcity — there are only so many workers, materials, machines,
  and hours in a day.
- Zimbabwe, Venezuela, and Weimar Germany all had sovereign currencies.
  They could print all they wanted. The result was not prosperity.

### "Taxes Drive Money" Gets Causation Backwards

MMT claims money has value because the government requires tax payments
in that currency. The Austrian response (via the regression theorem):

- Money originated through market processes — spontaneous emergence of
  the most marketable commodity as a medium of exchange.
- Government tax requirements can reinforce an already-established money
  but don't explain money's origin or value.
- Numerous currencies have lost value despite tax obligations denominated
  in them. If "taxes drive money," hyperinflation would be impossible
  in countries that levy taxes. It isn't.

### MMT Has No Coherent Inflation Theory

MMT proponents claim government can spend without limit until inflation
appears, then taxes should drain the excess. Problems:

- Inflation is not detectable in real time. Price effects of money creation
  are lagged and distributed unevenly across the economy.
- The assumption that the political system will raise taxes when needed
  ignores everything known about political incentives. Politicians who
  raise taxes lose elections.
- The model of inflation as "too much money chasing too few goods" without
  structural dimension ignores that new money distorts relative prices
  and the production structure. You can have asset price inflation,
  producer goods inflation, and consumer price stability simultaneously.
- By the time CPI inflation is politically undeniable, the structural
  damage (malinvestment, misallocated capital) is already done.

### The Job Guarantee Creates Employment, Not Value

MMT proposes the government as employer of last resort. The Austrian
response:

- Employment is not inherently valuable. What matters is whether the
  employment produces goods and services consumers actually want.
- Paying people to do things nobody would voluntarily pay for is
  resource consumption, not wealth creation.
- The "buffer stock" framing treats human beings as homogeneous,
  interchangeable units — the opposite of methodological individualism.
- It is Bastiat's broken window fallacy at scale, now rebranded as
  macroeconomic stabilization.

### MMT Ignores Capital Theory Entirely

There is no concept of the structure of production in MMT. No theory of
malinvestment. No account of how government spending distorts resource
allocation across industries and across time. The entire Austrian insight
about the temporal structure of capital is absent. MMT treats the economy
as a bathtub (fill with spending, drain with taxes) rather than as a
complex structure of heterogeneous capital goods arranged in temporal
sequences of production.

## Critique of Neoclassical Economics {#neoclassical}

### The Misuse of Mathematics

Neoclassical economics models the economy using differential equations,
constrained optimization, and general equilibrium theory. The Austrian
objections:

- **No constant quantitative relationships.** The "elasticity of demand"
  is not a constant — it changes with every shift in consumer preferences,
  knowledge, and circumstances. Writing it as a parameter in an equation
  implies a stability that doesn't exist.
- **Equilibrium is a mental construct, not a state the economy achieves.**
  General equilibrium models describe a state where all plans are mutually
  compatible and no further gains from trade exist. The real economy is
  always in disequilibrium, always adjusting, always discovering. The
  interesting economic phenomena happen in the process of adjustment,
  not in the hypothetical end-state.
- **"Perfect competition" is a contradiction.** The model of perfect
  competition assumes all firms sell identical products at identical
  prices with perfect information. But under these conditions, there is
  nothing left to *compete* about. Real competition is a dynamic process
  of entrepreneurial discovery, differentiation, and rivalry — exactly
  what the model assumes away.
- **Homo economicus is a caricature.** The rational utility-maximizer
  with complete information and stable preferences is a mathematical
  convenience, not a description of actual human action. Mises's
  "acting man" is broader: purposeful behavior under uncertainty, with
  changing preferences and incomplete knowledge.

### The Pretense of Measurement

- Utility cannot be measured (it's ordinal, not cardinal).
- "Social welfare functions" require interpersonal utility comparisons
  that are impossible in principle.
- Cost-benefit analysis in dollar terms smuggles in the assumption that
  dollar values accurately represent subjective valuations — they don't,
  because the marginal utility of money differs across people.

### What Neoclassical Economics Gets Right

Austrians acknowledge several neoclassical contributions:
- Supply and demand analysis (with Mengerian foundations)
- Marginal analysis broadly (though Austrians insist on ordinal, not
  cardinal, utility)
- The concept of opportunity cost
- Basic price theory

The disagreement is primarily methodological and about the proper limits
of economic knowledge, not about every substantive conclusion.

## The Subjective vs. Labor Theory of Value {#value-theories}

| Dimension | Labor Theory (Marx/Ricardo) | Subjective Theory (Menger/Mises) |
|-----------|---------------------------|----------------------------------|
| Source of value | Labor time in production | Individual subjective valuations |
| Direction of causation | Cost → Price | Price → Cost |
| Explains goods with no labor input? | No | Yes (scarcity + human need) |
| Explains valueless goods with much labor? | No | Yes (nobody values them at the margin) |
| Same good, different values to different people? | Cannot explain | Core feature |
| Role of consumer | Passive recipient | Sovereign — drives all production |
| Profit and interest | Exploitation (surplus value) | Return on time preference and entrepreneurial judgment |
| Labor heterogeneity | Circular ("skilled = multiple of simple") | Different labor valued by its marginal product |

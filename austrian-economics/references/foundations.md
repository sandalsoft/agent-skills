## Menger: Subjective Value and the Origin of Money {#menger}

### The Subjective Theory of Value

Carl Menger's *Principles of Economics* (1871) overturned the classical
cost-of-production theory of value. The argument:

**Against the labor theory of value:**
- Goods with no labor have value (a diamond found on the ground, fertile
  virgin land, a natural mineral spring).
- Goods with enormous labor can be worthless (a bad novel that took years
  to write, an elaborate sculpture nobody wants).
- The same good has different values to different people at the same moment
  (water to a person beside a river vs. someone dying of thirst).
- If labor determined value, these facts would be inexplicable.

**The positive theory:**
Value arises from the relationship between a human need and the available
supply of goods capable of satisfying that need. The value of any unit of
a good equals the importance of the least important satisfaction that would
be foregone if that unit were lost.

**The reversal of causation:**
Classical economics said: cost of production determines price. Menger showed
the reverse: anticipated consumer valuations determine what entrepreneurs
are willing to pay for factors of production. A coffee shop's rent isn't
high because the location is expensive — the location is expensive because
coffee shops (and other businesses) that locate there can earn high revenues
from consumers who value the convenience.

### Marginal Utility

People don't value "water in general" or "diamonds in general." They value
specific units given their current supply.

- Water is abundant; the marginal unit satisfies a trivial desire (watering
  the lawn). Low marginal value.
- Diamonds are scarce; the marginal unit satisfies a highly valued desire.
  High marginal value.

The water-diamond paradox dissolves. Nobody chooses between "all water" and
"all diamonds." They choose between incremental units.

### Orders of Goods and Imputation

**First-order goods** (consumer goods): bread, clothing, shelter — directly
satisfy human needs.

**Higher-order goods** (producer/capital goods): flour, ovens, labor — used
to produce lower-order goods.

The value of higher-order goods is *imputed* from the value of first-order
goods. Iron ore has value because steel has value because automobiles have
value because consumers want automobiles. The causal chain of value runs
from consumer to producer, never the reverse.

### Origin of Money

Menger explained money's emergence without government decree:

1. In barter, trade requires a double coincidence of wants (the fisherman
   who wants shoes must find a shoemaker who wants fish).
2. Some commodities are more marketable (widely accepted) than others.
3. Individuals discover they can facilitate trades by first acquiring a highly
   marketable commodity, then using it to buy what they want.
4. As more people adopt the most marketable commodity as a medium of exchange,
   its marketability increases further (network effect).
5. The process converges on one or a few commodities becoming money.

No collective decision. No government. Pure spontaneous emergence from
individual self-interest.

## Böhm-Bawerk: Capital, Time, and the Critique of Marx {#böhm-bawerk}

### Time Preference

People prefer present goods over future goods of equal kind and quality.
This is not merely about impatience — it reflects a structural feature
of human valuation:

- If people did not discount the future, they would never consume. They
  would always defer consumption to invest for greater future output.
  The fact that consumption occurs proves time preference exists.
- Interest is the market expression of time preference. Savers demand
  compensation for deferring consumption. Borrowers pay for present use
  of resources.
- The natural rate of interest reflects the community's aggregate time
  preferences.

### Roundabout Production

1. Direct production (picking berries by hand) yields output immediately
   but with low productivity.
2. Indirect production (building tools, then using tools to gather berries)
   takes longer but yields more per unit of input.
3. More roundabout methods are generally more productive, with diminishing
   returns.
4. The interest rate governs how roundabout production should be. Low time
   preference → more saving → lower interest rates → longer production
   processes become profitable → higher future productivity.

Capital goods are the physical embodiment of roundabout production. A factory
is a very roundabout way of making consumer goods. Its existence implies
someone deferred consumption to fund its construction.

**Capital is not homogeneous.** It consists of specific physical goods
(machines, half-finished products, buildings) arranged in a temporal
structure. This heterogeneity matters — a blast furnace cannot be
converted into a bakery without massive loss. This is why malinvestment
during a credit-fueled boom is so costly: the specific capital goods
created for projects that shouldn't have been started cannot easily be
redeployed.

### Critique of Marx's Exploitation Theory

Böhm-Bawerk's *Karl Marx and the Close of His System* demolished the
labor theory of value on multiple fronts:

1. **The transformation problem.** Marx admitted in Capital Vol. III that
   goods exchange at "prices of production" that systematically deviate
   from labor values. His attempted reconciliation is mathematically
   inconsistent — you cannot simultaneously maintain that total profit
   equals total surplus value AND total prices equal total labor values.

2. **Heterogeneous labor.** Marx treats labor as homogeneous, but an hour
   of surgery differs from an hour of ditch-digging. Marx's answer —
   skilled labor is a "multiple" of simple labor — is circular, since
   the multiple can only be determined from market wages.

3. **Interest as time price, not exploitation.** The capitalist advances
   present goods (wages) to workers in exchange for future goods (finished
   products sold later). The difference is compensation for waiting, not
   surplus extraction. Workers receive the present discounted value of
   their marginal product.

4. **Capital goods contribute to production.** A worker with a $50,000
   machine produces more than a worker with bare hands. The return to
   the machine's owner reflects the machine's real productivity.

## Mises: Praxeology and the Architecture of Economic Science {#mises}

### The Praxeological System

**The action axiom:** Man acts — employs means according to a plan to attain
ends. This is self-evidently true and irrefutable (any attempt to deny it
is itself an action).

**Categories deduced from the action axiom:**
- Means and ends: action implies selecting means for chosen ends
- Preference and choice: action implies preferring one state over another
- Cost: the most valued alternative foregone
- Time: action unfolds in time; the actor distinguishes sooner from later
- Uncertainty: the future is uncertain; if outcomes were certain, there
  would be no need to act
- Profit and loss: the actor expects improvement (psychic profit is the
  motive of all action); shortfall is loss

**Properties of praxeological laws:**
- A priori: known prior to experience (like logic)
- Synthetic: they describe reality (unlike tautologies)
- Apodictic: necessarily true; denial leads to contradiction
- Qualitative: describe directional relationships, never constants

### The Economic Calculation Problem (Detailed)

From "Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth" (1920) and
*Socialism* (1922):

1. Rational resource allocation requires comparing alternative uses.
2. Comparison requires a common denominator — money prices.
3. Money prices for capital goods arise only from exchange between
   independent owners.
4. Abolish private ownership of means of production → no exchange of
   capital goods → no prices for capital goods → no rational economic
   calculation.
5. The planning board cannot determine whether a production plan is
   economical or wasteful. It gropes in the dark.

**Why this is not a computational problem:**
The information that prices convey is *generated* by the market process.
It does not pre-exist the process. Asking a central planner to set correct
prices is asking someone to read a book that hasn't been written yet.

**The Lange-Mises debate:**
Oskar Lange proposed that socialist managers could mimic market pricing
through trial-and-error. Mises and Hayek responded:
- Real prices reflect constantly changing conditions, entrepreneurial
  discovery, and speculative judgment
- Bureaucratic trial-and-error lacks profit/loss incentives, genuine
  exchange between independent decision-makers, and speed of adjustment
- "Playing at markets" without actual property rights, actual profits,
  and actual bankruptcy is fundamentally different from having markets

### The Regression Theorem

**The circularity problem:** Money is valued for its purchasing power, but
purchasing power depends on people valuing money. Circular?

**Mises's solution (logical, not merely historical):**
1. Today's demand for money partly reflects yesterday's purchasing power
2. Yesterday's purchasing power reflected the prior day's demand
3. This regression terminates at the point where the money commodity was
   first used as a medium of exchange
4. At that point, it already had value for non-monetary uses (gold for
   jewelry, silver for industry)
5. Pre-existing commodity value provided the initial basis for monetary
   acceptance

**Implications:**
- Money cannot originate by pure fiat — a connection to commodity value
  is required at the origin (even if later severed)
- Money is not an arbitrary convention replaceable by anything
- This formalizes Menger's spontaneous emergence theory

### Methodological Dualism

Mises distinguished:

**Thymology:** Understanding of particular human valuations, motives, and
meanings. Historical and specific — tells us which ends people pursued
and why in particular situations.

**Praxeology:** Formal theory of action itself, independent of the content
of anyone's particular goals. Tells us the logical structure of all
purposeful behavior.

Economics (catallactics) is a branch of praxeology. It provides the "if-then"
structure. *If* credit expands beyond savings, *then* malinvestment results.
*How much* malinvestment and *where* it manifests depends on thymological
factors — the particular expectations and decisions of particular people —
which are not constant and not predictable.

### Impossibility of Interpersonal Utility Comparisons

Utility is subjective and ordinal (people rank preferences), not cardinal
(there is no unit of "utils"). You cannot say "Person A gains more utility
from this dollar than Person B." This demolishes the theoretical basis for
utilitarian redistribution arguments. It does not necessarily mean
redistribution is wrong — only that it cannot be justified by claiming to
maximize "total utility," because total utility is a meaningless concept
when utility cannot be measured or compared across persons.

## Hayek: Knowledge, Prices, and Spontaneous Order {#hayek}

### The Knowledge Problem

From "The Use of Knowledge in Society" (1945):

The fundamental economic problem is not allocating *given* resources
according to *known* preferences (the standard equilibrium framing).
The real problem: how does a society use knowledge dispersed among
millions of individuals, where no single mind possesses more than a
tiny fragment?

**The knowledge that matters:**
- Knowledge of particular circumstances of time and place — the shipper
  who knows about an empty vessel, the local merchant who knows about
  a temporary surplus. This knowledge is fleeting, local, and often tacit.
- This is not the kind of knowledge that can be centralized. Much of it
  exists as intuition and experience the holder cannot fully articulate.

**The price system as communication:**
1. Prices encode information. When tin becomes scarcer, its price rises.
   Users need not know *why* — they only need the price signal telling
   them to economize.
2. Prices compress the subjective valuations and local knowledge of
   millions into single numbers that guide behavior.
3. No central authority can replicate this, because the information that
   prices convey is generated by the exchange process itself.

### Spontaneous Order

**Taxis** (designed order): An army, a bureaucracy — created by deliberate
design for a specific purpose.

**Kosmos** (spontaneous order): A market, a language, common law — emerges
from human action but not human design. No one planned it. No one controls
it. Yet it exhibits complex functional order.

Spontaneous orders outperform designed orders when the problem involves
coordinating many people with dispersed knowledge and diverse purposes.
Designed orders work for small-scale, well-defined problems.

### The Pretence of Knowledge

Hayek's 1974 Nobel lecture distinguished simple phenomena (physics, where
relevant variables are few enough for quantitative prediction) from complex
phenomena (markets, ecosystems, language — where only qualitative pattern
predictions are possible).

Economics can predict that rent ceilings produce shortages. It cannot predict
by how much or when. Pretending otherwise — the "pretence of knowledge" —
leads to disastrous policy because planners act on false precision.

### The Political Argument Against Planning

From *The Road to Serfdom* (1944):

1. Central planning requires agreement on a comprehensive hierarchy of
   ends — a single value scale for allocating all resources.
2. Democratic deliberation cannot produce such agreement in a diverse society.
3. The planning authority must therefore impose its value scale — requiring
   concentrated coercive power.
4. Those most willing to exercise such power tend to rise to the top.
5. Economic control is inseparable from personal freedom. If the state
   controls all employment, housing, and production, dissent becomes
   materially impossible.

This is structural, not a slippery slope. Partial planning tends toward
more comprehensive planning because unplanned sectors create "problems"
planners feel compelled to address.

## Additional Austrian Concepts {#additional}

### The Cantillon Effect

New money enters the economy at specific points and affects prices
sequentially, not uniformly:
- First recipients (banks, government contractors) spend at pre-adjustment
  prices — gaining real purchasing power
- As money diffuses through the economy, prices rise progressively
- Last recipients (workers, retirees, the poor) face higher prices before
  their incomes adjust
- Monetary expansion systematically redistributes from the politically
  disconnected to the politically connected

### Entrepreneurship

**Mises:** The entrepreneur bears genuine uncertainty (not merely calculable
risk). Entrepreneurial profit arises from correctly anticipating future
consumer wants better than competitors. Losses arise from misjudging them.
This cannot be formalized or delegated to algorithms because it involves
judgment about non-repeatable situations.

**Kirzner:** Entrepreneurial alertness — the ability to notice previously
unperceived opportunities. Competition is a dynamic discovery process, not
the static "perfect competition" of textbooks where all information is
given. The entrepreneur's role is to discover information that the
equilibrium model simply assumes everyone already has.

### Why Austrians Reject Macroeconomic Aggregates

1. **GDP conceals production structure.** It doesn't distinguish between
   an economy producing $1T of consumer goods vs. $200B consumer + $800B
   capital goods. These are radically different structures.
2. **GDP counts government spending as production.** Digging and filling
   holes raises GDP. This conflates wealth creation with resource consumption.
3. **CPI obscures relative price changes.** Credit expansion doesn't raise
   all prices equally. It changes ratios — capital to consumer goods, assets
   to goods, wages across industries. A single index hides these distortions.
4. **Aggregation destroys causal information.** If investment rises $100B
   while consumption falls $100B, aggregate demand is unchanged — but the
   economic meaning (shifted time preferences, lengthening production
   structure) is profound.
5. **"The price level" is not an operational concept.** Different prices
   move at different times, magnitudes, and for different reasons. A
   weighted average is a statistical artifact, not a causal force.
6. **Aggregates don't act.** "The economy" doesn't decide to grow.
   Individuals decide. Causal explanations must trace to individual action.
